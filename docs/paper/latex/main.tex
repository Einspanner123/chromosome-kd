\begin{abstract}
Chromosome karyotyping --- the microscopic inspection of metaphase
chromosomes for genetic diagnosis --- is a slow, labor-intensive, and
observer-dependent procedure in clinical cytogenetics. Each metaphase
cell contains roughly $46$ densely packed chromosomes across $24$
morphologically similar classes; automating this analysis requires a
detector accurate and fast enough for routine clinical deployment.
Anchor-based detectors plateau in fine-grained intra-group
discrimination, while diffusion-based detectors inherit the slow many-step
inference and trajectory truncation errors of their image-generation
ancestors, and small clinical datasets further destabilize training.
We present \emph{KaryoFlow}, a diffusion-based chromosome detector
organized into generation, interaction, and decision stages. Rectified Flow
(RF) provides the adopted box-generation foundation and is the dominant
accuracy source; an RF-adapted data-prediction form of DPM-Solver++ reduces
four-step inference from seven to four network evaluations while matching
Heun accuracy on the same checkpoint. Our independent contribution is
Localization-Quality Calibrated Ranking (LQCR): a final proposal feature
predicts localization quality and re-ranks emitted detections by $s=pq^2$
without changing classes, coordinates, proposal renewal, or solver dynamics.
On the 24 Chromosomes Object validation set, a strict fixed-checkpoint
final-only ablation improves mAP from $0.86301$ to $0.87044$ and improves
AP90/AP95 by $0.03041/0.03251$. We further expose a detection-specific
inconsistency between multistep history and proposal renewal: disabling renewal
preserves proposal identity and changes three-seed mAP by only $-0.0003$ on
Dataset~2 and $-0.0030$ on the distribution-shifted Dataset~1 at $K=500$,
while reducing latency by $2.43\%$. Cascade-head distillation compresses six
heads to three (24 to 12 cascade-head evaluations, solver NFE unchanged) for a
$1.73\times$ speedup with $-0.004$ mAP. A frozen conditional-step policy gives
$13.79\%$ lower latency at $-0.0007$ mAP relative to fixed two-step inference.
Controlled coupling experiments show no reliable accuracy benefit for
Stochastic Coupling. All empirical claims are limited to two chromosome
datasets; detector-generic implications follow from stated proposal-ranking
and multistep-state assumptions rather than unperformed COCO experiments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Chromosome karyotyping --- the visual analysis of metaphase chromosomes for
genetic disease diagnosis --- is a labor-intensive clinical task. Each
metaphase image contains about $46$ tightly packed chromosomes across $24$
classes (A1--Y), with severe class imbalance, fine-grained intra-group
similarity, and frequent overlaps. Automating this process requires a
detector that is both accurate and fast enough for clinical deployment, yet
the main families of methods developed over the past decade each leave
specific limitations. Anchor-based detectors (Cascade R-CNN,
YOLOX-S) reach high throughput but plateau in fine-grained
24-class discrimination that distinguishes the acrocentric C-group
chromosomes from one another. Transformer detectors (DINO) lift accuracy
but rely on multi-scale deformable attention and heavier backbones,
which is less suitable when clinical data are scarce. Diffusion-based detectors
(DiffusionDet) reformulate detection as iterative denoising from noisy
boxes and match the structured-prediction nature of the task, but inherit the slow many-step inference and
curved-trajectory truncation errors of their DDPM ancestors and are
further destabilized by the small training sets typical of clinical
imaging.

Rectified Flow~\cite{liu2023rectified} (RF) addresses these issues by
replacing the curved stochastic DDPM trajectory with a deterministic
straight-line ODE path from noise to ground truth, enabling few-step
inference with low truncation error; errors accumulate across proposals
when per-image object density is high ($\sim 46$ boxes). Applying RF to
\iffalse
detection raises three questions: how should noise samples be coupled to
ground-truth boxes when the prediction space is low-dimensional and the
target count per image is large? Which ODE solver delivers the best
accuracy--latency trade-off in the few-step regime? And how can training
be stabilized on the imbalanced, small-scale corpora that characterize
medical imaging? We address these issues --- coupling design,
solver selection, and training stability --- in turn.

We make three contributions, each tied to a specific difficulty of chromosome
detection and validated on two public datasets (Chromosome20240904, $1{,}540$ images;
and 24 Chromosomes Object, $5{,}000$ images) with multi-seed experiments,
per-class AP analysis, test-set evaluation, and an FPS benchmark.

The first difficulty is the accuracy ceiling of existing detectors on
fine-grained 24-class chromosome imagery, where anchor-based designs
struggle with dense packing and intra-group morphological similarity. Our
\emph{KaryoFlow} detector addresses this by adopting the RF training
paradigm, achieving $+0.082$ \mAP{} over the Euler baseline on 24
Chromosomes Object and $+0.017$ over DDPM on the original dataset. In
comparison with SOTA detectors (experimental data in
Section~\ref{sec:exp-sota}), KaryoFlow as a diffusion-based detector
approaches the transformer-based SOTA DINO R50 ($47$M parameters): the
overall \mAP{} gap is $0.63\%$ ($0.863$ vs $0.868$),
exceeding Cascade R-CNN, and improving over the
DDPM-based DiffusionDet ($+0.060$ \mAP{} at seed~42 best, $+0.056$ at the
3-seed mean). Although DINO R50 remains statistically better
under a per-image paired Wilcoxon test, RF as a diffusion-based method has
narrowed the gap with transformer-based SOTA. Because the
DDPM-baseline$\to$RF+Heun comparison changes several variables at once
(DDPM$\to$RF, Euler$\to$Heun, $1\to4$ steps), we further conduct a
solver$\times$step disentanglement ablation that attributes $94\%$ of the
gain to the RF paradigm and only $6\%$ to solver and step-count choices;
AdaLN-Zero contributes null individually (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}). The
accuracy gain therefore originates from the paradigm itself
rather than from solver tuning.

The second difficulty is a training pathology induced by optimal-transport
(OT) coupling in the low-dimensional detection space. When $d=4$ and
$K \approx 46$, deterministic OT assignment collapses coupling diversity
toward zero --- a failure mode we formally analyze as \emph{OT Diversity
Collapse} (upper bound $\Delta H \le \log K$, matched lower bound
via Fano's inequality, empirically tight to within $0.03\%$) --- and
degrades training, especially when data are scarce. We propose
\emph{Stochastic Coupling}, which samples assignments from the Sinkhorn
transport matrix rather than taking an argmax, restoring coupling
diversity. The remedy is most effective where clinical data are scarcest:
on the smaller Dataset~1 it yields a significant \mAP{} gain
($+0.034$, $p<10^{-120}$) that diminishes with dataset size, and on both
datasets it reduces within-run epoch-level \mAP{} oscillation by
$4.6\times$.

The third difficulty is inference latency: the curved, many-step
trajectories of DDPM-based detectors are incompatible with interactive
clinical screening. We deploy DPM-Solver++~\cite{lu2022dpmpp} for
four-step RF inference ($1.71\times$ speedup over Heun, \mAP{} $0.859 \pm
0.003$ over three seeds), combined with Top-$K$ pruning to reach
12.9--14.5~FPS in the retained operating range. A per-image paired ablation reveals DPM-Solver++ yields a
statistically significant precision advantage over Heun at
matched step count ($+0.006$ per-image \mAP{}, Wilcoxon $p<10^{-6}$,
Table~\ref{tab:stat-tests}), refining FlowDet's~\cite{baty2025flowdet}
conclusion that higher-order solvers perform worse: at matched steps the
higher-order solver is slightly \emph{better}.

Relative to the closest prior work:
FlowDet~\cite{baty2025flowdet} (CFM with mini-batch OT, reports
higher-order solvers perform worse), we contribute the formal
analysis of \emph{why} mini-batch OT becomes a liability in
low-dimensional structured prediction (Section~\ref{sec:method-ot}) and
a solver$\times$step disentanglement showing that, at matched step count, the
higher-order DPM-Solver++ \emph{improves} over Heun
(contradicting FlowDet's report of strict inferiority),
while being $1.71\times$ faster in NFE.
Relative to DeFloMat~\cite{deflomat2025} (RF for medical
detection, coupling as implementation detail), we provide the formal
analysis of coupling design as a training pathology and the Stochastic
Coupling remedy. Unlike OT-CFM~\cite{tong2023conditional} and multisample flow
matching~\cite{pooladian2023multisample} (high-dimensional image generation,
$d \sim 10^5$), our setting is low-dimensional ($d=4$) with $K \approx 46$,
where OT collapse is severe ($\Delta H/H \approx 0.69$). AdaLN-Zero~\cite{dhariwal2021diffusion}
is reused as a standard implementation detail (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs});
DPM-Solver++ is adopted off-the-shelf, our contribution being the controlled
disentanglement analysis with per-image paired statistical tests.

detection raises three separable questions: how accurately can the box path be
learned, how cheaply can it be integrated, and how should fixed detections be
ranked when class confidence is misaligned with localization quality? These
correspond to the generation, numerical-integration, and decision stages in
Figure~\ref{fig:method_overview}.

Our first contribution is an evidence-controlled RF detection formulation.
RF is adopted from prior generative modeling, but we isolate its role in the
four-dimensional box space and show that it accounts for the dominant gain
over the DDPM detector on both chromosome datasets. This establishes a strong
generative detection foundation without claiming RF itself as a new algorithm.

Our second contribution is the RF data-prediction adaptation of
DPM-Solver++~\cite{lu2022dpmpp}. DPM-Solver++ is also adopted, while the
detection-specific derivation and implementation reduce four-step inference
from seven Heun evaluations to four. Same-checkpoint evaluation shows matched
accuracy ($-0.001$ aggregate mAP), so its defensible contribution is efficiency
rather than an intrinsic precision gain.

Our main independent accuracy contribution is Localization-Quality Calibrated
Ranking (LQCR). The probability-ranking principle factors the true-positive
posterior into class correctness and localization survival. LQCR predicts a
continuous localization-quality proxy from the last proposal feature and uses
$s=pq^2$ only for final output ranking. The strict final-only implementation
keeps solver states, renewal, Top-$K$, boxes, and predicted classes unchanged,
making the measured gain causally attributable to ranking. On Dataset~2 it
adds $0.00743$ mAP and improves AP90/AP95 by $0.03041/0.03251$ on the fixed
seed-42 A4 basis. Its formulation is detector-generic; broader COCO validation
and clean paired multi-seed replication remain required for a universal claim.

Beyond the accuracy head, we contribute an evidence-backed inference analysis.
DPM-Solver++ uses a finite-difference correction between adjacent predictions;
renewing a proposal between those predictions changes its identity, so the
history term is no longer a derivative estimate along one trajectory. Removing
renewal restores the mathematical premise of the multistep update. At the
retained proposal count this is effectively accuracy-neutral on both datasets
and removes $2.43\%$ latency, while the observed failure at $K=100$ explicitly
bounds the claim. Head distillation and geometry-aware conditional stepping
then expose two orthogonal efficiency axes---cascade depth and per-image solver
steps---with measured $1.73\times$ and $13.79\%$ speed benefits, respectively.

Our third methodological result concerns prediction parameterization. Although
$x_0$- and velocity-prediction are information-equivalent, their squared losses
differ by $t^{-2}$. Direct data prediction avoids this low-$t$ amplification
under the shifted schedule and is $+0.002$ mAP better than velocity prediction
on each dataset's three-seed validation mean. This is a small but replicated
optimization result, not a claim that data prediction is universally superior.

Finally, standardized multi-seed experiments falsify the earlier hypothesis
that Stochastic Coupling is an accuracy contribution. Random, Hard-OT, and
Sinkhorn-sampled coupling are statistically indistinguishable within the
controlled pipeline. We report this negative result and use Random coupling in
the clean mainline, preventing an unsupported OT narrative from inflating the
method definition.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\iffalse
\paragraph{Diffusion-based object detection}
Diffusion-based detectors reformulate detection as iterative denoising from
noisy boxes to GT boxes. \emph{DiffusionDet}~\cite{chen2023diffusiondet} relies on DDPM, whose curved
stochastic trajectories make few-step inference slow and
truncation-error-prone. \emph{DiffuBox}~\cite{chen2024diffubox} extends
diffusion to 3D detection, but its point-diffusion refinement paradigm is
fundamentally different from our 2D noise-box-to-GT formulation and cannot
be directly borrowed. \emph{FlowDet}~\cite{baty2025flowdet} is closest to
our work---it uses Conditional Flow Matching with mini-batch OT coupling,
but has two limitations: it reports ``higher-order solvers perform
worse'' without analyzing why, and overlooks the harm of OT coupling in
low-dimensional detection spaces. \emph{DeFloMat}~\cite{deflomat2025} applies
Rectified Flow to medical detection but treats coupling as an implementation
detail, ignoring its role in low-data regimes. Our work addresses these
limitations: we formally analyze the OT diversity collapse pathology in
low-dimensional spaces, answering the ``why'' question FlowDet left
unaddressed; we show via a controlled solver disentanglement that
higher-order solvers are slightly \emph{better} at matched step
count, correcting FlowDet's conclusion; and we analyze coupling design
as a training pathology and
propose Stochastic Coupling as a remedy.

\paragraph{Rectified Flow and Flow Matching}
Rectified Flow~\cite{liu2023rectified} replaces curved DDPM trajectories with
straight-line ODE paths, and Flow Matching~\cite{lipman2023flow} provides a
unified training framework. Existing OT coupling methods (OT-CFM~\cite{tong2023conditional},
multisample flow matching~\cite{pooladian2023multisample}) work well in
\emph{image generation}---a high-dimensional ($d \sim 10^5$) setting where
$K$ equals batch size and OT loss is negligible. Detection differs
fundamentally: the dimensionality is extremely low ($d=4$) and
each image contains many targets ($K \approx 46$).
\emph{FlowDet}~\cite{baty2025flowdet} and \emph{DeFloMat}~\cite{deflomat2025}
also work in this low-dimensional setting, but neither recognized the OT
diversity collapse: FlowDet only reports empirical OT results, and DeFloMat
treats coupling as an implementation detail. Our key insight is that
precisely in this low-dimensional, high-$K$ regime, OT coupling approaches
its $\log K$ entropy-reduction upper bound and coupling diversity collapses
to zero. This failure mode is neither addressed by OT-CFM and other
high-dimensional works (where OT loss is negligible) nor equivalent to the
concurrent analysis of Cheng and Schwing~\cite{cheng2025curse} on conditional
high-dimensional generation degradation (their failure mode is
condition-skewed prior, orthogonal to our low-dimensional collapse). We
analyze this pathology and propose Stochastic Coupling as an
interpolating remedy between hard OT and random pairing, an explicit
solution for OT collapse in low-dimensional structured prediction. Beyond
detection, flow matching has been applied in biomedical imaging:
Jones et al.~\cite{jones2026flowmatching} characterize the flow-matching design
space for cellular microscopy, and Nützel et al.~\cite{nutzel2026flowmatching}
apply subclass-conditioned flow matching to medical image augmentation. On
the TMI venue specifically, Yang et al.~\cite{yang2025dgddm} and Shen
et al.~\cite{shen2025ladine} demonstrate diffusion models for cross-domain
medical image segmentation and robust classification, respectively.

\paragraph{Diffusion and flow-based object detection}
DiffusionDet~\cite{chen2023diffusiondet} formulates detection as iterative
box denoising. FlowDet~\cite{baty2025flowdet} and
DeFloMat~\cite{deflomat2025} replace DDPM-style dynamics with conditional
flow matching or RF, but do not isolate localization-aware final ranking.
Our use of RF follows this literature; the new component is a causally
isolated quality-ranking head that changes only the ordering of emitted
detections. DiffuBox~\cite{chen2024diffubox} addresses 3D refinement and is
architecturally distinct from the 2D noise-box setting considered here.

\paragraph{Rectified Flow, numerical solvers, and quality ranking}
RF~\cite{liu2023rectified} and Flow Matching~\cite{lipman2023flow} provide the
adopted continuous-time generation framework. DPM-Solver++~\cite{lu2022dpmpp}
provides the adopted multistep integration principle; our method adapts its
data-prediction update to the RF box path. Unlike coupling-based flow-matching
methods such as OT-CFM~\cite{tong2023conditional}, LQCR targets the downstream
probability-ranking problem after coordinates have already been generated.
This separation is important because the standardized coupling experiments
in our setting are null, whereas the final-ranking intervention produces a
controlled high-IoU AP gain.

\paragraph{Chromosome detection}
Automated chromosome detection has been studied for decades, originally
through classical image-processing pipelines (thresholding, morphology,
watershed) that require per-cohort parameter tuning and break down under
staining variation, overlaps, and banding-pattern noise. Modern
learning-based approaches adopt general-purpose detectors: YOLO and
Faster R-CNN~\cite{ren2015faster} variants reach high throughput but
plateau in the fine-grained 24-class discrimination that clinical
karyotyping demands, because anchor priors and dense prediction heads are
not tailored to the morphologically similar intra-group chromosomes
(C6--C12, F-group) that dominate residual errors. Transformer detectors
such as DINO improve accuracy but rely on multi-scale deformable attention
and heavier backbones, which is less suitable when clinical training data are scarce
($1{,}540$--$5{,}000$ images per cohort). ChromosomeNet~\cite{kuo2024chromosomenet}
uses the same Taichung dataset as our 24 Chromosomes Object benchmark but
releases no code or pretrained models; recent CNN classifiers focus on
the downstream classification step assuming boxes are already given,
sidestepping the detection problem we address. Diffusion-based detection
has not been applied to chromosome karyotyping in prior work, though
a concurrent work uses a restoration diffusion network for chromosome
anomaly augmentation~\cite{zhang2026perturb}. We
close this gap with an open-source diffusion-based detector for
chromosome karyotyping (code released upon publication), validated with
multi-seed experiments and per-class AP analysis that exposes where the
remaining errors concentrate (small chromosomes and the morphologically
similar C-group).

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Rectified Flow for Detection (KaryoFlow)}\label{sec:method-rf}

\paragraph{RF formulation}
The conditional probability path is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rf-path}
  \mathbf{x}_t = (1-t)\, \mathbf{x}_0 + t\, \mathbf{x}_1, \qquad t \in [0,1],
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}_0$ is the target (GT bbox) and $\mathbf{x}_1$ is the source (Gaussian
noise). The corresponding velocity field is constant along the path of~(\ref{eq:rf-path}),
$\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_0$, and the training objective is the flow matching loss in~(\ref{eq:fm-loss})
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fm-loss}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}} = \E_{t, \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1}\!\left[ \norm{ \mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) - (\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_0) }^2 \right].
\end{equation}
\emph{Detection-specific adaptation}: source $\mathbf{x}_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},
\sigma^2 \mathbf{I}_4)$, target $\mathbf{x}_0$ are GT bboxes, $d=4$ (vs.\ $d=196{,}608$ in
image generation), and $K \approx 46$ targets per image.

\paragraph{Time conditioning}
AdaLN-Zero~\cite{dhariwal2021diffusion} serves as the time conditioning
mechanism, using zero-initialized adaptive layer norm so the network starts
as an unconditional model (identity at $t{=}0$). A separate ablation
(Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}) confirms its individual contribution to \mAP{}
is null; the $+0.082$ \mAP{} gain is entirely attributable to RF, with the
shifted noise schedule also contributing negligibly. We retain AdaLN-Zero as
a standard implementation detail.

\subsection{ODE Solvers}\label{sec:method-solver}

\paragraph{Heun solver (2nd-order)}
Heun's method provides 2nd-order ODE accuracy via the predictor--corrector in~(\ref{eq:heun}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heun}
\begin{aligned}
  \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-\Delta t} &= \mathbf{x}_t + \Delta t \cdot \mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t), \\
  \mathbf{x}_{t-\Delta t}       &= \mathbf{x}_t + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\,[ \mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) + \mathbf{v}_\theta(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-\Delta t}, t{-}\Delta t) ].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\emph{Cost}: 2 network forward evaluations (NFE) per step. At 4 steps this yields 8 NFE (7 in practice;
the last step degrades to Euler).

\paragraph{DPM-Solver++ (2nd-order multistep, 1 NFE/step)}
We adapt DPM-Solver++~\cite{lu2022dpmpp} to the RF linear path. The model
directly predicts $\mathbf{x}_0$ (the GT box), and the update uses polynomial
interpolation of the $\mathbf{x}_0$ history in $t$ space (not the log-SNR
$\lambda$ space of VP-SDE). The RF-ODE in data-prediction form is
$d\mathbf{x}/dt = (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0(t))/t$; integrating exactly over
$[t_n, t_{n+1}]$ with linear $\mathbf{x}_0(t)$ interpolation yields the update in~(\ref{eq:dpmpp})
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dpmpp}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathbf{x}_{t_{n+1}} &= \tfrac{t_{n+1}}{t_n}\,\mathbf{x}_{t_n}
    + \bigl(1 - \tfrac{t_{n+1}}{t_n}\bigr)\,\mathbf{x}_0^{(n)}
    + \varphi_1\,\mathbf{D}_1, \\
  \varphi_1 &= t_{n+1}\log\tfrac{t_n}{t_{n+1}} - t_n + t_{n+1},\;
  \mathbf{D}_1 = \tfrac{\mathbf{x}_0^{(n)} - \mathbf{x}_0^{(n-1)}}{t_n - t_{n-1}},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where the first two terms are the exact solution for constant $\mathbf{x}_0$
and $\varphi_1 \mathbf{D}_1$ is the 2nd-order correction for linearly varying
$\mathbf{x}_0(t)$. The singularity at $t{\to}0$ is handled by an $\epsilon$
cutoff ($t_{n+1} > 10^{-7}$); a 3rd-order variant adds a quadratic term
$\varphi_2 \mathbf{D}_2$. Unlike VP-SDE DPM-Solver++, $\mathbf{x}_1$ (the
initial noise) is a fixed sample and is \emph{not} interpolated; its
contribution is carried implicitly by $\mathbf{x}_{t_n}$.
\emph{Cost}: 1 NFE per step. At 4 steps this yields 4 NFE (vs Heun's 7).

\paragraph{Why direct data prediction is numerically preferable here}
For the path in~\eqref{eq:rf-path},
$\mathbf{x}_0=\mathbf{x}_t-t\mathbf{v}$. Hence the two parameterizations carry
the same information, but their pointwise squared errors satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:x0-v-weight}
 \mathcal{L}_{v}(t)=t^{-2}\mathcal{L}_{x_0}(t).
\end{equation}
With the shifted schedule, velocity regression therefore amplifies gradient
noise as $t\rightarrow0$, where accurate terminal boxes matter most. Direct
$x_0$ prediction removes this avoidable conditioning factor. The predicted
$+0.002$ validation advantage is reproduced by three seeds on each dataset;
test differences remain within noise, so the result supports stable
optimization rather than universal statistical dominance.

\paragraph{Solver type is \mAP{}-neutral on RF+Heun}
Evaluating the RF+Heun checkpoint across all solver$\times$step combinations
(Table~\ref{tab:solver-ablation}), solver
type has \emph{no effect on \mAP{}} at matched steps (Euler = DPM-Solver++ at
both 4-step and 1-step). Step count has marginal effect ($+0.004$ from 1 to 4
steps). Heun's $+0.001$ over Euler 4-step costs $1.75\times$ NFE (7 vs 4),
not cost-effective. A second same-checkpoint check on the A4 model gives
$0.863$ for four-step DPM-Solver++ and $0.864$ for four-step Heun
($\Delta=-0.001$). The solver is therefore an efficiency component rather
than an accuracy component.

\paragraph{Conclusion}
DPM-Solver++ is an efficiency component. On the same A4 checkpoint at four

\subsection{History-Consistent and Compressed Inference}
\label{sec:method-engineering}

\paragraph{Proposal identity is a premise of multistep history}
Let $r_i^n=1$ indicate that proposal row $i$ is renewed after step $n$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:dpmpp} assumes that
$\mathbf{x}_{0,i}^{(n)}$ and $\mathbf{x}_{0,i}^{(n-1)}$ describe the same
proposal trajectory. If $r_i^n=1$, the former is evaluated from a newly drawn
state while the latter belongs to the discarded state. Consequently
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{D}_{1,i}=
 \frac{\mathbf{x}_{0,i}^{(n)}-\mathbf{x}_{0,i}^{(n-1)}}{t_n-t_{n-1}}
\end{equation}
is a secant between different identities and cannot approximate
$d\mathbf{x}_{0,i}/dt$ along either trajectory. This is not a chromosome-
specific argument: it applies to any multistep proposal solver that replaces
rows without resetting row-wise history. We therefore disable box renewal
after confirming that proposal redundancy is sufficient in the retained
$K\geq200$ regime. The $K=100$ counterexample, where mAP drops by $0.0313$,
shows the exact boundary: solver consistency does not compensate for an
insufficient proposal pool.

\paragraph{Cascade-head distillation}
Solver NFE counts network calls, whereas each call evaluates $H$ cascade heads.
We distill a six-head teacher into a three-head student using head mapping
$\{0\!\to\!0,1\!\to\!2,2\!\to\!5\}$ and feature/prediction supervision. At
four solver steps this reduces cascade-head evaluations (CHE) from 24 to 12,
while NFE remains four. This distinction corrects the earlier, inaccurate
``24 to 12 NFE'' description. The compressed model reaches $0.859$ versus
$0.863$ and reduces RTX A6000 latency from $77.57$ to $44.72$ ms.

\paragraph{Conditional computation}
Geometry-aware adaptive conditional stepping (GACS) freezes convergence and
confidence thresholds on seed~42, then chooses one or two steps per image.
It is an optional speed policy rather than the accuracy model: on Dataset~1 it
uses $1.6136$ mean steps, exits early on $38.64\%$ of images, and reduces
latency by $13.79\%$ at $-0.0007$ mAP versus fixed two-step inference. Its mAP
remains below the four-step reference, so we do not call it lossless relative
to the production setting.

\subsection{Localization-Quality Calibrated Ranking}\label{sec:method-lqcr}

For prediction feature $F$, class-correctness event $C$, localization IoU $U$,
and evaluation threshold $\tau$, the true-positive posterior factorizes exactly:
\begin{equation}
 P(C{=}1,U{\geq}\tau\mid F)
 =P(C{=}1\mid F)P(U{\geq}\tau\mid C{=}1,F).
 \label{eq:lqcr-factorization}
\end{equation}
The probability-ranking principle therefore favors ordering detections by the
joint class--localization posterior, rather than class confidence alone. LQCR
predicts $q\approx\mathbb{E}[U\mid C{=}1,F]$ from the final proposal feature and
uses $s=pq^2$ as a low-cost cross-threshold surrogate. If the conditional IoU
distributions form a one-parameter stochastically ordered family, $q$ preserves
the ordering of all threshold-survival probabilities; this is a Bayes-consistent
ranking surrogate under that assumption, not a universal theorem that maximizes
COCO AP for arbitrary IoU distributions.

Our strict final-only implementation keeps raw class scores in the solver,
proposal renewal, Top-$K$ pruning, and early stopping. Calibration is applied
only when emitting detections, so boxes, predicted classes, and RF trajectories
remain fixed. Figure~\ref{fig:lqcr-principle} summarizes the principle, causal
boundary, and fixed-seed controlled effect.

\subsection{OT Diversity Collapse and Stochastic Coupling}\label{sec:method-ot-legacy}

\paragraph{Voronoi partitioning by OT}
\begin{lemma}[OT $\to$ Voronoi]\label{lem:voronoi}
When $N \to \infty$, OT coupling partitions $\R^d$ into $K$ Voronoi cells
$\mathcal{V}_k = \{\mathbf{z} : \norm{\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{b}_k} \le \norm{\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{b}_j},\; \forall j \ne k\}$,
assigning each $\mathbf{z}_i$ to its nearest GT box.
\end{lemma}
This is the standard semi-discrete OT limit~\cite{santambrogio2015optimal}.

\paragraph{Setup}
Let source $\nu = \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_d)$ (noise; effective std at time $t$
is $\sigma_t = t\sigma$ along the RF path) and target
$\mu = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \delta_{\mathbf{b}_k}$ ($K$ GT boxes). A coupling assigns
each noise sample $\mathbf{z}_i$ to a target box $\mathbf{b}_{V_i}$. We let
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,topsep=2pt,itemsep=0pt]
  \item $V \in \{1,\ldots,K\}$: coupling assignment RV (which GT box a noise sample is paired with),
  \item $X_t = (1-t)\, \mathbf{b}_V + t\, \mathbf{z}$: the flow state observed by the model,
  \item $H(V \mid X_t)$: conditional entropy of $V$ given $X_t$ --- how much $X_t$ leaks about $V$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{proposition}[OT Diversity Upper Bound]\label{prop:ot-gap}
Under the Voronoi partitioning assumption (Lemma~\ref{lem:voronoi},
requiring $N \to \infty$) and the Gaussian noise model,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ot-upper}
  \Delta H \;=\; H_{\text{rand}}(V \mid X_t) - H_{\text{OT}}(V \mid X_t) \;\le\; \log K.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch (full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}).]
Under random coupling, $H_{\text{rand}}(V \mid X_t) \le H(V) = \log K$ in the
high-noise regime. Under OT coupling with $N \to \infty$, $V =
\operatorname{Voronoi}(\mathbf{z})$ is deterministic (Lemma~\ref{lem:voronoi}), so given
$X_t$ one recovers $\mathbf{z}$ and hence $V$, giving $H_{\text{OT}}(V \mid X_t) = 0$.
Therefore $\Delta H \le \log K$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Empirical validation (Chromosome20240904)}
$\Delta H = 3.8415$, $\log K = 3.8427$ ($K_{\text{mean}} = 46.6$), relative
error $0.03\%$. Figure~\ref{fig:ot-theory} visualizes the partitioning and the
empirical match, and Figure~\ref{fig:entropy-phase} traces conditional entropy
$H(V \mid Z)$ as a function of the stochastic coupling parameter $\epsilon$.

\paragraph{Tight lower bound}
The upper bound~(\ref{eq:ot-upper}) of Proposition~\ref{prop:ot-gap} is essentially tight:
a matching lower bound shows OT collapse is severe whenever noise is small
relative to inter-box distances.

\begin{proposition}[OT Diversity Lower Bound]\label{prop:ot-lower}
Under the Gaussian noise model with variance $\sigma_t^2 I_d$ at time $t$
and well-separated GT boxes ($\min_{j \ne k} \norm{\mathbf{b}_k - \mathbf{b}_j} \ge d_{\min}$),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ot-lower}
  \Delta H \;\ge\; \log K \cdot \bigl(1 - P_{\mathrm{err}}(t)\bigr)
  - h\bigl(P_{\mathrm{err}}(t)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $P_{\mathrm{err}}(t) \le \binom{K}{2}\, \Phi\!\bigl(-d_{\min}/(2\sigma_t)\bigr)$
is the nearest-neighbor misclassification probability (union bound) and
$h(\cdot)$ is the binary entropy. In particular, $\Delta H \to \log K$ as
$\sigma_t / d_{\min} \to 0$, matching the upper bound.
\end{proposition}

For chromosome detection ($d_{\min} \approx 20$\,px, $\sigma_t \sim 1$\,px),
$P_{\mathrm{err}} < 10^{-45}$, so the bound~(\ref{eq:ot-lower}) predicts $\Delta H \ge 0.999\,\log K$,
consistent with the observed $0.03\%$ gap and confirming that OT collapse is
intrinsic to the low-dimensional regime rather than an artifact of the $N \to \infty$ idealization.

\paragraph{Stochastic Coupling via Sinkhorn transport}
\begin{definition}[Stochastic Coupling]\label{def:stoch}
Instead of an argmax assignment, we sample the coupling from the Sinkhorn
transport matrix rows~\cite{cuturi2013sinkhorn}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stoch-coupling}
  \pi_{\text{stoch}}(i) \;\sim\; \operatorname{Categorical}\!\left( \frac{ T_\epsilon(i,:) }{ \sum_j T_\epsilon(i,j) } \right).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
The key property of the coupling in~(\ref{eq:stoch-coupling}) is that $H_{\text{stoch}}(V \mid X_t; \epsilon)$ increases
monotonically with $\epsilon$; endpoints are $\epsilon \to 0$ = hard OT and
$\epsilon \to \infty$ = random coupling.
\begin{proposition}[Stochastic Coupling Monotonicity]\label{prop:monotone}
Under uniform source marginal, the Stochastic Coupling of Definition~\ref{def:stoch} satisfies that $H_{\text{stoch}}(V \mid X_t; \epsilon)$ is
monotonically non-decreasing in the Sinkhorn regularization $\epsilon \ge 0$
(proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}, via the envelope theorem applied to
entropy-regularized OT).
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Coupling Audit}\label{sec:method-ot}

We evaluated Random, Hard-OT, and Sinkhorn-sampled coupling under the same
augmentation and evaluation protocol. Although deterministic assignment can
reduce pairing diversity in the four-dimensional box space, the controlled
multi-seed experiments do not show a reliable mAP advantage for stochastic
coupling on either chromosome dataset. Coupling is therefore treated as an
auxiliary design choice and negative-result analysis, not as a component of
the final method or an accuracy contribution. The complete model uses Random
coupling to remove this confound.

\subsection{Top-$K$ Proposal Pruning}\label{sec:method-topk}
After step 0 of inference, we prune proposals from 500 to $K$ based on
confidence scores; only the top-$K$ proposals proceed through steps 1--3.
DPM-Solver++ compatibility requires \texttt{dpm\_solver.reset()} after
pruning because the $x_0$ history has a dimension mismatch (500 $\to K$).

\paragraph{Detection protocol}

random-noise proposals are iteratively denoised; with time-ensemble,
predictions from all steps are concatenated and deduplicated by per-class
NMS (IoU $0.5$). Evaluation uses COCO mAP$@0.5{:}0.95$ (10 IoU thresholds,

intervention, so its ranking effect is isolated from training and trajectory

chromosome analysis, without the multi-scale deformable attention of DINO or

RTMDet-L ($0.742$) and DINO R50 ($0.737$), suggesting the diffusion paradigm
is competitive in low-data small-target regimes.

\subsection{Controlled Coupling Result}\label{sec:exp-coupling}

With standardized augmentation, the three coupling strategies are equivalent
within cross-seed variation on both datasets. On Dataset~1, their three-seed
means lie within approximately $0.002$ mAP; on Dataset~2, the stochastic-minus-
random difference is also non-significant. Earlier larger gains pooled
incompatible experimental lineages and are superseded. We retain this result
to document the falsified direction, while excluding Stochastic Coupling from
the model definition and contribution list.

\subsection{Solver Analysis}\label{sec:exp-solver}

\paragraph{DPM-Solver++ step ablation}
DPM-Solver++ converges at 2 steps (\mAP{} $0.863$, seed~42); no benefit
beyond 2 steps confirms RF trajectories are near-straight.

\paragraph{DPM-Solver++ vs Heun at matched NFE}
At similar NFE, DPM-Solver++ 4-step (4 NFE, $0.863$) $\approx$ Heun 2-step
(3 NFE, $0.863$) --- equal accuracy, so DPM-Solver++ achieves $43\%$ fewer NFE
at no cost. At matched \emph{step count} on the same A4 checkpoint,
DPM-Solver++ gives $0.863$ and Heun gives $0.864$ ($\Delta=-0.001$), which
is practically neutral. We therefore attribute no accuracy gain to the
off-the-shelf solver; its value is the lower evaluation count and latency.

\paragraph{Test set evaluation}
On the Dataset~2 test set, DPM-Solver++ achieves \mAP{} $0.859$ (vs val
$0.863$ for seed~42, $\Delta = -0.004$), confirming that the aggregate
accuracy generalizes well. The AP$_S$ point estimate, however, swings from
$0.499$ (val) to $0.577$ (test) for the same checkpoint, indicating that
AP$_S$ on this 24-class benchmark is high-variance (only $60$ val images
contain small objects) and should be interpreted with confidence intervals
from the final clean paired runs rather than as a point estimate.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented \emph{KaryoFlow} and separated generative detection into
generation, integration, and final decision. RF is the adopted accuracy
foundation, while RF-adapted DPM-Solver++ matches Heun accuracy with four
rather than seven network evaluations. LQCR is the independent task-specific
contribution: under strict final-only isolation, localization-quality ranking
improves Dataset~2 mAP by $0.00743$ and AP90/AP95 by
$0.03041/0.03251$ without changing boxes, classes, or trajectories.
Same-hardware measurements reach $12.9$ FPS with DPM-Solver++ and $14.5$ FPS
with Top-$K$ ($K=200$). The completed engineering analysis supplies additional
independent value: history-consistent inference removes $2.43\%$ latency with
negligible high-$K$ accuracy change across both datasets; head distillation
gives a $1.73\times$ speedup at $-0.004$ mAP; and optional conditional stepping
reduces two-step latency by $13.79\%$ at $-0.0007$ mAP. Direct $x_0$ prediction
also matches the theoretical avoidance of $t^{-2}$ gradient amplification and
beats velocity prediction by $0.002$ validation mAP on both datasets.

The evidence audit also changes what we do \emph{not} claim. Standardized
multi-seed experiments do not support Stochastic Coupling as an accuracy or
stability contribution, and same-checkpoint tests do not support an intrinsic
DPM-Solver++ precision gain. The empirical scope is two chromosome datasets
with substantially different distributions and a ResNet-50 backbone. The
renewal, ranking, and parameterization analyses are detector-generic at the
level of their stated mathematical assumptions, but this is theoretical
transferability, not empirical COCO evidence. Resource limits precluded a
complete COCO study; mini-COCO is the next external validation. LQCR also
requires clean paired multi-seed replication, and $pq^2$ remains a monotone
surrogate under an ordered conditional-IoU assumption rather than a universal
COCO-AP optimum.

\section{Proofs and Additional Ablations}\label{app:proofs}
For LQCR, Equation~\eqref{eq:lqcr-factorization} follows directly from the
chain rule of conditional probability. If the family
$P(U\mid C{=}1,F;\eta)$ is first-order stochastically ordered by scalar
$\eta$, then both $\mathbb{E}[U\mid C{=}1,F]$ and
$P(U\geq\tau\mid C{=}1,F)$ are non-decreasing in $\eta$ for every $\tau$.
Thus a calibrated estimate $q$ of conditional mean IoU is a consistent
ordering proxy for each threshold-survival term. The exponent in $pq^2$ is a
calibration choice and does not alter this monotonicity argument; it is not a
proof of optimal COCO AP under arbitrary conditional IoU distributions.

latency uses one RTX A6000 at $512\times512$, batch size one, with CUDA-event
